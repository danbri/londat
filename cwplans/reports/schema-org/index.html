Docklands schema.org harvest
cwplans · web harvest · report of 2026-10-06

The schema.org data that Docklands businesses publish about themselves

What we extracted from the websites of the occupants, brands and charities that the Canary Wharf registry links to: the entities, the search features the markup targets, shape patterns we can check, and how many descriptions refer to the same real thing.

Summary

  • We hold 65,566 quads from 824 pages: 455 live pages on 378 websites, and 369 Internet Archive copies of the canarywharf.com directory. Most of the markup is site furniture that SEO plugins write (WebSite, WebPage, BreadcrumbList, SearchAction).
  • The useful entities are organisations (Organization on 161 sites) and local places (LocalBusiness and its subtypes on 109 sites, 209 place descriptions). Only 69 of the 209 place descriptions have a name, a structured address, a telephone number and opening hours together.
  • The markup targets Google's organisation, local business and breadcrumb features, and a retired feature (the sitelinks search box, on 111 sites). Open Graph tags for social link previews are on almost every page.
  • About 16 recurring idioms (small groups of triples, such as a branch card, an organisation card, weekly hours or a breadcrumb trail) carry nearly all of the content. Each idiom appears in a few known forms, for example an address as text or as a PostalAddress node. Open shapes describe each idiom and its forms, so we can recognise it in any page and rewrite it into one canonical form for the graph.
  • The 960 entity descriptions reduce to about 421 distinct entities (130 organisations, 291 places). Most of the repetition is one organisation repeated on each page of its own site. The cross-site matches are few (13) and come from shared @id IRIs, social profiles and telephone numbers.
  • Eight groups put one web description against two or three registry buildings. These are candidate registry faults: mostly mall occupants that the registry attaches to more than one mall building.

The canarywharf.com directory (369 archived pages)

These pages carry only the WordPress and Yoast furniture: WebPage, WebSite, BreadcrumbList and the publisher (Canary Wharf Group). They do not type the shop or restaurant. The business shows only in the page name ("Caffé Nero at Jubilee Place | Canary Wharf"), the URL path (/restaurant/, /shop/, /see-do/), the section (Shops & Services 171 pages, Eat & Drink 161, What's On 6) and the dates (datePublished from 2013, dateModified to 2024). The dates are useful as "listed since" and "last edited" signals for each occupant.

Who wrote the markup

The @id patterns show the tools. Yoast-style IRIs (#website, #organization, #breadcrumb, /#/schema/) are on 43 to 87 sites; the WordPress REST link is on most WordPress pages; theme microdata is on 22 sites. Chains use store-locator platforms (for example locations.pret.co.uk), and one restaurant group publishes the same IRIs through a booking platform (mildreds.getbento.com). So most markup is a plugin default, not written by the business. This explains why site furniture is everywhere and place facts are rare.

Descriptive idioms (shapes)

Sites say the same things with the same small groups of triples. Each group is an idiom: a node of one type, its usual properties, and the child nodes that hang from it. We describe each idiom once as an open shape. The shape recognises the idiom and its known forms in any page; it does not reject data. A recognised idiom is then rewritten into one canonical form for the knowledge graph, and the original form is kept as provenance.

The idioms below were mined from the data: for each typed node we took its properties and the types of its child nodes (two levels), grouped equal patterns, and counted the sites that use each one.

The variation is in form, not in meaning: an address as a text string or as a PostalAddress node; hours as a text string ("Mo-Fr 07:00-19:00") or as nodes; a day as "Monday" or as schema:Monday; a logo as an IRI or an ImageObject; the country as GB, UK, England or United Kingdom. Each form is one alternative in the idiom's shape, and each has one rewrite to the canonical form.

  s:name . ? ;
  s:telephone . * ;
  s:url . ? ;
  ( s:address @<Address> | s:address LITERAL ) ? ;
  s:geo @<Geo> ? ;
  ( s:openingHoursSpecification @<Hours> *
  | s:openingHours LITERAL * ) ;
  ( s:parentOrganization @<OrgCard>
  | s:brand . | s:branchCode . ) *
}

  s:postalCode . ? ; s:addressRegion . ? ;

Rewrites: parse a text address into PostalAddress parts (postcode first); expand text hours into one node a day; map day names to schema: IRIs; map country names to ISO codes; turn a logo IRI into an ImageObject. Each rewrite is small and runs only on its own form.

How the shapes are used

  • Recognise. A ShEx shape map over each page graph tags each node with the idioms it takes part in, and with the form. This gives an index of "what this page says" (a branch card, an organisation card, hours, a menu) without reading the triples.
  • Normalise. Each form of an idiom has one rewrite to the canonical form. In SHACL this is a rule (SHACL Advanced Features sh:rule with a SPARQL CONSTRUCT); in ShEx it is a mapping from the matched shape to a template. Either way, the original triples stay in the page's named graph.
  • Set aside furniture. The site identity, page frame, search box, breadcrumb and social preview idioms are plugin output. Recognising them lets us keep their useful facts (site name, page dates, section path) and drop the rest from the entity layer.
  • Compare sites. The form counts show which publishers write which forms. For example, 24 sites write the address as text and 35 write hours as text. A store-locator platform writes the same form for every branch, so one rewrite covers a whole chain.
  • Grow the set. New idioms come from the same mining: a pattern that recurs on several sites and is not yet described is a candidate shape.

How many descriptions refer to the same thing

Method: take every node that is an organisation or a place (960 after removing navigation markup). Join them with keys in order of strength, and union the joined nodes into groups. Telephone and sameAs joins are allowed only between nodes of the same kind (organisation with organisation, place with place), because an operator and its venue often share a phone number and social profile.

Kinds of repetition found

  • The same organisation on every page of its own site. This is the largest kind: Canary Wharf Group alone is 369 descriptions (one for each archived directory page). Site plus normalised name collapses it.
  • One business on two of its own domains or a platform. Same @id IRI on mildreds.com and mildreds.getbento.com (6 IRIs), atis.life and atisfood.com, barrys.com and barrysbootcamp.com, socialpubandkitchen.co.uk and sportsbarandgrill.co.uk.
  • One branch on its store page and on the brand's store finder. Pret: two branches with the same IRI and telephone on locations.pret.co.uk and pret.co.uk.
  • Brand profiles. Charles Tyrwhitt on charlestyrwhitt.com and ctshirts.co.uk, joined by six shared social and Wikipedia links.

Where the keys fail

  • Shared central numbers. Two Post Office branches (Churchill Place, Cubitt Town) share one telephone number. Vision Express and David Clulow share an 0800 number. Telephone must not join branches of a chain without a second key (postcode or coordinates).
  • Empty values. An empty sameAs string joined 13 unrelated sites in the first run. Drop empty and bare-domain social URLs before you join.
  • Registry conflicts. 8 groups put one web description against two or three registry buildings. Examples: Smilepod (cwb-0466, cwb-0586), Nando's (cwb-0411 Cabot Place, cwb-0586 East Wintergarden), Vision Express (cwb-0411, cwb-0466, cwb-0586). These are mall occupants attached to more than one mall building. They belong in the fault register as one error class, not as single fixes.

Estimating at a larger scale

  • Blocking and union. Group by cheap keys (IRI, normalised site and name, postcode, a 50 m geohash cell, telephone), then union the groups. This is the method above; it scales to millions of nodes.
  • Precision by sampling. Label a random sample of 100 merged pairs by hand for each key. The share that is right is the precision of that key; it sets the key's weight.
  • Missed links by capture and recapture. Two independent keys (for example IRI and sameAs on one side, telephone plus postcode on the other) find two sets of true pairs. With n1 and n2 pairs found and m found by both, the total is about n1 × n2 / m (Lincoln–Petersen). This gives an estimate of the pairs that no key finds.
  • Scored matching. For pairs inside one block, add log-likelihood weights for agreement on name, postcode, coordinates within 30 m, telephone and host (Fellegi–Sunter). Set two thresholds: link, and send to a person.
  • Agreement with the registry. Each page carries the registry keys that led us to it. On average one registry key leads to 1.76 web entities (usually the branch and its brand). A group that spans two buildings, or a key that leads to two different places, is a fault to check.
